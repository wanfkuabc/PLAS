Abstract
Referring image segmentation aims to accurately localize
001
and segment target instances based on natural language
002
descriptions. Although multimodal large language mod-
003
els possess powerful visual-language understanding ca-
004
pabilities, a significant gap remains between their high-
005
dimensional semantic representations and the fine-grained
006
spatial structures required for pixel-level segmentation,
007
limiting the effective translation of semantic understand-
008
ing into precise segmentation. To address this, this pa-
009
per proposes a Progressive Latent Alignment framework
010
for Referring Image Segmentation (PLAS), which achieves
011
progressive optimization from multimodal semantic under-
012
standing to pixel-level segmentation through masked la-
013
tent representation learning, cross-modal feature align-
014
ment, and segmentation task adaptation. Specifically, a
015
teacher model—comprising a mask encoder, an auxiliary
016
reconstruction branch, and a SAM decoding branch—is first
017
constructed to learn masked latent representations that cap-
018
ture spatial structures and exhibit robustness to noise. Sub-
019
sequently, latent space alignment and auxiliary mask re-
020
construction supervision guide the multimodal large lan-
021
guage model to map multimodal semantic features into the
022
target mask latent space. Finally, leveraging latent space
023
constraints and pixel-level segmentation supervision, the
024
prompt adaptation module and SAM decoder are jointly op-
025
timized to effectively transform latent representations into
026
fine-grained segmentation masks. This framework decou-
027
ples semantic representation learning from segmentation
028
task adaptation through progressive training and eliminates
029
the need for the teacher model or auxiliary reconstruction
030
branches during inference.

2. Related Work 102
2.1. Referring image segmentation 103
RIS has evolved from convolutional language–vision fu- 104
sion models to systems built on multimodal large language 105
models. Early transformer-based methods use language- 106
conditioned attention or cross-modal fusion to predict a 107
dense mask, including VLT and LAVT [4, 30]; CLIP-driven 108
alignment provides another strong formulation [27]. Subse- 109
quent work studies generalized or weakly supervised set- 110
tings, including multi-target and null-target expressions, 111
text-only supervision, and zero-shot transfer [6, 12, 14, 15, 112
34]. More recent approaches reuse the generative interface 113
of an MLLM: the model emits a special segmentation token, 114
a sequence of polygon coordinates, or a set of spatial mask 115
tokens that is interpreted by a subsequent decoder. These 116
formulations benefit from the broad semantic prior of the 117
language model, but they place the burden of spatial recon- 118
struction on the language output space. They also use dif- 119
ferent output units for semantic supervision and geometric 120
supervision, which can make optimization sensitive to tok- 121
enization, coordinate length, and the resolution of the output 122
grid. 123
2.2. MLLM-based mask and prompt decoding 124
MLLM-based segmenters generally follow one of two pat- 125
terns. The first extends the language vocabulary with a spe- 126
cial token whose hidden state is mapped to a mask embed- 127
ding, as in LISA and GSVA [10, 29]; PixelLM and GLaMM 128
further use pixel-oriented codebooks or grounding modules 129
for multi-target reasoning [22, 23]. The second asks the 130
model to generate explicit spatial entities such as polygons 131
or a grid of foreground tokens, as explored by SAM4MLLM 132
and Text4Seg [3, 11]. Both patterns keep semantic reason- 133
ing and mask generation in a single autoregressive stream. 134
Promptable foundation segmenters offer a complementary 135
alternative: an image encoder stores dense visual features 136
and a lightweight prompt encoder and mask decoder turn 137
points, boxes, or learned dense prompts into masks [9]. Our 138
method combines these strengths. The MLLM resolves the 139
referring expression, while the latent–prompt adapter trans- 140
lates its aligned spatial representation into a dense prompt 141
for the image-conditioned decoder. 142
2.3. Latent alignment for dense prediction 143
Latent supervision has been used to transfer information 144
between networks, but the target representation is often a 145
global embedding or an intermediate feature with no ex- 146
plicit reconstruction contract. PLAS uses a different inter- 147
face: the teacher latent is obtained directly from the tar- 148
get mask and is jointly constrained by two decoders. The 149
image-free head forces the latent to retain mask geometry 150
without relying on the image, and the SAM path forces it 151

3. Method
166
3.1. Problem formulation and overview
167
Given an RGB image I and a referring expression e, re-
168
ferring image segmentation (RIS) predicts a binary mask y
169
for the object described by e. The visual–language model
170
produces a semantic, token-level representation, whereas
171
segmentation requires a dense and spatially precise out-
172
put. PLAS bridges these spaces with a compact normalized
173
mask latent and a three-stage teacher–student training pro-
174
cedure.
175
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the method contains a mask-
176
latent teacher, a multimodal student, and a unified latent–
177
prompt bridge F. The teacher defines a geometrically
178
meaningful latent target from the ground-truth mask. The
179
student then predicts this target from I and e, after which
180
the predicted latent is converted into a dense prompt for
181
SAM. The three stages separate latent construction, latent
182
prediction, and image-conditioned mask adaptation, which
183
keeps the target representation stable while the student is
184
optimized.
185

4. Experiments 304
4.1. Experimental setup 305
Datasets. We evaluate on four standard referring segmen- 306
tation benchmarks. RefCOCO and RefCOCO+ contain 307
short expressions collected for referring-expression com- 308
prehension, with RefCOCO+ suppressing explicit location 309
words; RefCOCOg contains longer, compositional descrip- 310
tions [21, 32]. We also evaluate on gRefCOCO, which ex- 311
tends the setting to multiple referred objects and expres- 312
sions with no valid target [14]. We use the conventional val- 313
idation and test splits: val, testA, and testB for RefCOCO 314
and RefCOCO+, val and test for RefCOCOg, and val, testA, 315
and testB for gRefCOCO. 316
Metrics. For RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg, 317
we report cumulative IoU (cIoU), computed as the cumu- 318
lative intersection divided by the cumulative union over the 319
evaluation set. For gRefCOCO, we report both cIoU and 320
generalized IoU (gIoU), following the benchmark conven- 321
tion. cIoU emphasizes aggregate pixel accuracy, while gIoU 322
averages the per-expression IoU and therefore reflects per- 323
formance across examples with different object sizes. All 324
values are percentages; higher is better. 325
Training details. We train PLAS on six NVIDIA 326
RTX 4090D GPUs, each with 24 GB of memory. The per- 327
device batch size is 1, and gradients are accumulated over 328
16 steps, yielding an effective batch size of 96 under data- 329
parallel training. We clip the global gradient norm to 2.0. 330
The MLLM is adapted with parameter-efficient LoRA fine- 331
tuning while the original Qwen weights remain frozen, fol- 332
lowing the low-rank adaptation strategy of Hu et al. [5]. We 333
use LoRA rank r = 64, scaling factor α = 128, dropout 334
0.05, and no bias term. 335
Comparison protocol. The PLAS row is obtained from 336
the experiments described in Sec. 3. Results for prior meth- 337
ods are transcribed from their reported benchmark tables. 338

5.4. Efficiency–performance trade-off 410
In addition to segmentation quality, we evaluate the infer- 411
ence cost of the compared systems. Figure 4 plots cIoU 412
against the per-image inference time under the measure- 413
ment protocol used to produce the figure. PLAS with the 414
1024-token configuration is located in the upper-left corner 415
of the plot: it reaches approximately 85.7 cIoU while re- 416
quiring about 0.5 s per image. This is a favorable Pareto 417
position compared with methods that require substantially 418
longer decoding times while obtaining lower or comparable 419
cIoU. 420
The efficiency comes from the compact latent interface. 421
At inference, PLAS predicts one spatial mask latent from 422
the image–expression pair and converts it into a dense SAM 423
prompt. It therefore avoids iterative mask-token generation 424
and uses the frozen image encoder only once per image. 425
The result is a short inference path that preserves the strong 426
segmentation accuracy reported in Tables 1 and 2. The tim- 427
ing comparison is hardware and implementation dependent, 428
so the figure is used to characterize the relative efficiency– 429
accuracy trend under the same evaluation protocol. 430
